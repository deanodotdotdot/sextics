This example begins with the sparse holdout chain, with coefficients 1/2, and adds

\frac13\cdot X_0^2\cdot X_1\cdot X_2\cdot Y_0\cdot Y_2.

The target remains X_0^3\cdot Y_0^3, and every mixed term still has X/Y-bidegree (4,2). The added term breaks the separate paired sign changes in the first two pairs, while preserving their product and the third paired sign change. It is a controlled loss of symmetry rather than a generic dense perturbation.

The Fermat-cover construction identifies a second, more direct obstruction to reusing the holdout proof. With T^6=Y_0^6+Y_2^6, \alpha=Y_0/T, \beta=Y_2/T, and y=Y_1, the added term becomes

\frac13\cdot\alpha\cdot\beta\cdot X_0^2\cdot X_1\cdot X_2\cdot T^2.

It is not invariant under y\leftrightarrow T. Thus the equation no longer descends through S=y^2+T^2, P=yT to the weighted quotient used by that construction. Maintaining the same bidegree and central target is insufficient to maintain the transfer.

The complementary quotient in Chapter 2, §2.8 is also unavailable unchanged: the added term has weight four under its diagonal weights (5,1,5) on Y. Preserving the two-link bidegree does not preserve either of these serving quotients.

This makes the instance a useful comparison with the cyclic coupling: both produce an unmatched T^2 term on this cover, but only the cyclic example retains the full paired-sign group. One can therefore ask whether the obstruction is best addressed through a replacement cover, a different quotient, or a method insensitive to these sign characters. A failed correction to the existing conic does not exclude such alternatives.

The mixed-biweight deformation supplies the complementary comparison: its paired signs survive, but its cover acquires an unmatched fourth power. Together these entries separate symmetry of the physical sextic, symmetry after base change, and the target-density identities needed to return a certificate.

The cyclic cubic construction uses a different quotient from the chain cover, but still relies on paired signs to write the equation as a cubic in X_i^2 after fixing Y_i/X_i. The offending term here is odd under simultaneous negation of X_0,Y_0, so that substitution does not produce the same three-square cubic. Thus the new Jacobian route does not remove this example's original symmetry obstruction.
