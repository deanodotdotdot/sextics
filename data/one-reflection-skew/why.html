The sextic of One reflection · residual sector has hidden exchanges of Y_0 and Y_1, which allow its target to split between two cubic-surface constructions. The additional term

\frac17\cdot X_0\cdot X_1\cdot Y_0\cdot Y_1\cdot(Y_0^2-Y_1^2)

breaks those exchanges while preserving the prescribed involution and the separate Y_2 reflection. The target X_0^3\cdot Y_0^3 is even in Y_2, so that surviving reflection does not serve it. Smoothness follows from a direct derivative estimate: the mixed terms cannot cancel the pure sixth-power derivative at a coordinate of maximal modulus.

The equal-coefficient cover gives a checked construction for the transverse target on the unperturbed equation. Its useful reflection does not survive this skew deformation. The same choice T^6=Y_0^6+Y_2^6, Y_0=\alpha T, Y_1=y transforms the skew term into

\frac17\cdot\alpha\cdot X_0\cdot X_1\cdot y\cdot T \cdot(\alpha^2T^2-y^2).

It is not invariant under y\leftrightarrow T over the Fermat coefficient field. Thus neither the original exchange nor this particular cover supplies the desired quotient. The successful cover argument suggests a direction, but its hypotheses still need to be re-established for this fixed sextic.

A nonsingular replacement conic solves the analogous curve problem for the ternary deformation, where the quotient survives. That example supplies reusable lifting and integration machinery, but does not cure the missing exchange here: the equation must first be placed on a serving auxiliary geometry.

The instance isolates a small deformation of an explicit construction without changing the target or introducing a generic dense equation. A solution could extend the cubic-surface method through the skew coupling, find a different cover with a serving quotient, or construct support for the residual sector by another route. Failure of the two specified reductions is not a claim that all reductions or all supporting divisors have been excluded.
