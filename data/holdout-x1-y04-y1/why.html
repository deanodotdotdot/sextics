The target on the holdout sextic is

A=X_1\cdot Y_0^4\cdot Y_1.

It uses the first two coordinate pairs and omits the endpoint pair. Compared with the central X_0^3\cdot Y_0^3, it transfers degree toward Y_0 while including both middle coordinates. Its small variable support makes it a useful test of the difference between a sparse numerator and a separated equation.

The tempting interpretation of Y_0^4 as a passive multiplier fails at the level of the equation. The first mixed term contains X_1\cdot Y_0\cdot Y_1, and the second joins the middle pair to X_2,Y_2. A field in the apparent active pair still has to account for both couplings. The certificate cannot be obtained merely by multiplying a field from a binary example and ignoring the resulting derivative terms.

The monomial is invariant under each paired sign change and under the endpoint exchange. The exchange reverses the residue volume, so its quotient serves the opposite residue sector. The larger direct quotient calculation likewise leaves this target outside its 48-dimensional supported span in the 58-dimensional paired-sign sector. These observations delimit the direct quotient arguments; they do not imply that the class lacks support.

The central holdout construction offers a way to retain those interactions. On its Fermat cover, a new exchange produces a weighted quotient whose moving-conic family integrates polynomial sources. This supplies reusable geometry, but does not automatically identify the source for every monomial on the original sextic.

For this target the substantial Y_0-dependence and the middle factor Y_1 must both be incorporated into the source and its normal derivative. One must then recover the physical density by trace, with no unaccounted contribution from differentiation in the coefficient directions. This is a concrete test of the difference between extending an integration theorem and merely multiplying a previously constructed field.
