The certificate formulation turns a support problem into polynomial identities. Computation can verify an explicit construction, expose an unexpected solution, or test a proposed mechanism. This chapter explains the complete coefficient system, the limits of numerical discovery, and the exact statement a submitted proof must establish.

5.1 Fixing a denominator makes the problem linear

For fixed F, q, A and a homogeneous denominator D of degree d, the numerator components have degree d+6\cdot q-5 and the prescribed parity. The normalized equations are

\begin{gathered} q\cdot dF(N)=A\cdot D,\\ D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N)-N(D)=F^q\cdot C. \end{gathered}

Here C has degree 2\cdot d-6. Introducing its full coefficient space makes both equations linear in N and C. A negative degree means C=0; at d=3, C is a Y-odd constant and also vanishes. Thus a normalized certificate with d\le 3 is necessarily strict. Exact linear algebra therefore decides whether this particular D serves this particular A, with the full numerator space. Allowing A to vary computes the target image at that pole bound. To obtain the complete target image supported on V(D), use denominator D^q for 1\le q\le 4; D alone can miss targets when q=2. At invariant q=1, passing to the Jacobian quotient separates nonzero primitive directions from polynomial corrections. At q=2 it records only the leading Hodge-graded piece, and lower-pole contributions must be retained.

For a candidate supporting divisor, this gives a deterministic target-map computation without a separate geometric resolution. A geometric calculation can explain its source and image; the coefficient system supplies an independent algebraic test. The prescribed-support theorem in Chapter 1 proves that this complete coefficient test and geometric support have the same image when the stated pole bound is used.

Denominator multiplicity and presentation redundancy must be distinguished. Multiplying both N and D by the same polynomial changes no rational field. Searching all numerators over D^{m} instead changes the allowed pole order. At q=1 the prescribed-support theorem proves that this cannot rescue a denominator rejected by the complete numerator system over D. At q=2 it can: D^{2} may be necessary. Chapter 1 gives the precise statement.

5.2 Solving E_{1} does not solve E_{2}

Write R_F=S/J(F). Since q is invertible, E_{1} has a polynomial solution precisely when A\cdot D\in J(F), or equivalently [A][D]=0 in R_F. Taking homogeneous components of a Jacobian representation gives exactly the required numerator degree; averaging gives the required parity. Thus E_{1} is an exact linear preprocessing step, with no guessed shape for D.

The Hilbert and trace series proved in Chapter 4 give the following dimensions. For any odd sextic A, multiplication by [A] has even-denominator source and odd target:

Denominator degree d \operatorname{dim} (R_F)_d^{+} \operatorname{dim} (R_F)_{d+6}^{-} Forced kernel dimension
9 623 623 0
10 762 468 At least 294
11 843 333 At least 510
12 885 208 At least 677

The numbers are coefficients of the explicit polynomials H(t) and \Theta (t), so they apply to every smooth F. A nonzero Jacobian-ring class [D] gives a representative coprime to F, since F\in J(F) by Euler's identity and F is irreducible. Consequently E_{1} always admits a legal even denominator of degree ten. This does not solve E_{2}.

There is also a uniform formula for E_{1} alone. Put H=\operatorname{Hess}(F). Differentiating Euler's identity gives H\cdot Z=5\cdot\nabla F and hence \operatorname{adj}(H)\cdot\nabla F=(\operatorname{det} H)\cdot Z/5. For every degree-six A,

D_{0}=\operatorname{det} H, N_{0}=\frac{5}{6}\cdot\operatorname{adj}(H)\cdot\nabla A \Longrightarrow N_{0}(F)=A\cdot D_{0}.

The final equality uses Z\cdot \nabla A=6\cdot A. It is a polynomial identity even where H is singular. A search based on it must still check legality and E_{2}; the formula alone is not a certificate theorem.

The full correction map

Fix an E_{1} denominator D of degree d and one lift N_{0}. The regular sequence of partial derivatives has its first syzygies generated by the Koszul relations. Thus every other E_{1} lift is N_{0}+K, where

\begin{gathered} K=\sum _{i<j}c_{ij}(F_j\partial _{i}-F_i\partial _{j}),\\ \operatorname{deg} c_{ij}=d+6\cdot q-10, F_i=\partial _{i}F. \end{gathered}

The coefficients c_{ij} can be restricted to the characters making the vector field legal; they still give every legal syzygy by averaging. Define B_D(N)=D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N)-N(D). Differentiating K makes its Hessian terms cancel pairwise, so

T_D(c)=B_D(K) =\sum _{i<j}\Bigl(D\cdot[(\partial _{i}c_{ij})\cdot F_j-(\partial _{j}c_{ij})\cdot F_i]-c_{ij}\cdot(D_i\cdot F_j-D_j\cdot F_i)\Bigr).

Every term belongs to J(F). E_{2} is therefore equivalent to the finite affine membership problem

-B_D(N_{0}) \in F^q S_{2\cdot d-6} + \operatorname{im} T_D \;\text{inside}\; S_{2\cdot d+6\cdot q-6},

with the specified parity throughout. This proves the complete correction criterion, including its independence of the initially selected lift. The quotient by this image is the full obstruction; replacing it by the Jacobian quotient gives only a necessary condition.

5.3 Eliminating linear variables without discarding solutions

When D varies, E_{1} can be solved jointly in its coefficients and those of N. After exact gauge removal, a q=1 presentation often takes the form

\begin{gathered} D=U_D\cdot a, N=U_N\cdot a+K\cdot z,\\ r(a,z)=B(a,a)+C(a,z)=0. \end{gathered}

The vector a parametrizes denominators and z retains every numerator-only fibre direction. The residual r is the full E_{2} map. Since simultaneous scaling changes no rational field, a projective chart or normalization is required. The chosen chart must keep D nonzero modulo F; normalizing only the combined numerator/denominator vector can approach a spurious numerator-only point at infinity.

Exact E_{1} elimination and verified gauge quotients preserve the complete solution set in the declared degree and parity. Restricting D to chosen factors, contact forms, or auxiliary symmetry characters changes the search space. Such restrictions can test a geometric prediction, but their failure cannot settle the unrestricted degree. Global Y-evenness is the required parity; invariance under additional symmetries of F is a separate restriction.

Eliminating numerators may also yield only necessary equations in D. Such equations can be useful: a candidate passing them is retained for full numerator reconstruction. Their solution set may contain false positives. Generic points of that larger set need not find the exceptional subset that completes E_{2}, so an expansion is not automatically a complete search method. Rank strata of the retained numerator map matter.

5.4 From numerical candidates to exact coefficients

Variable projection solves for the best z at each trial a. It can reduce the optimization dimension, but near a rank drop its minimum-norm solution can change sharply. A singular-value cutoff can then change the effective problem. Restore the complete chosen numerator space and test any candidate in the original equations.

A small coefficient residual is useful for discovery, but is not a certificate. It depends on scaling and on the chosen presentation of N/D. Monitor whether D is approaching zero modulo F, and use independent evaluations of the rational residual to help distinguish a promising candidate from a degenerate one. Failure of an optimizer to converge is not a proof that the coefficient system is inconsistent.

Once a candidate is stable, reconstruct its coefficients over \mathbb Q or a specified number field. For a reconstructed denominator, solve the complete numerator system again in exact arithmetic. If it is inconsistent, the numerical candidate has not produced a certificate; the reconstruction or the proposed denominator must change. If it is consistent, retain the actual numerator tuple and the quotient from E_2, then check the identity below independently.

Exact incidence lifting

A useful alternative to reconstructing a whole rational field is to reconstruct a small piece of its supporting geometry. Let R be an integral coefficient ring, including the fixed coefficients of the hypersurface and any endpoint relations. Let E_1,\ldots,E_m\in R[u_1,\ldots,u_m] be a square incidence system, and let \Delta=\det(\partial E_i/\partial u_j). A point over a field with E_i=0 and \Delta\ne0 proves that

S=\bigl(R[u_1,\ldots,u_m]/(E_1,\ldots,E_m)\bigr)[1/\Delta]

is nonzero. Its invertible Jacobian makes it étale and hence flat over R. Since R is a domain, passage to its fraction field keeps this nonzero algebra nonzero. A field factor of the generic étale algebra is a finite separable extension of that fraction field, carrying a solution and the required nonvanishing determinant. Additional open conditions may be inverted if they hold at the seed. An injective extension of the generic coefficient field, such as passage to complex coefficients, preserves the argument.

This is the mechanism behind the modular quartic of §3.3 and the replacement conic of §2.7. It does not say that an arbitrary point modulo a prime lifts, or that an arbitrary change in the equation preserves a solution. The exact equation must be defined over the integral base before reduction; square nonsingularity, flatness and the generic-field injection are the bridge. The characteristic-zero coupling stays fixed, even when its modular reduction is simpler.

A lifted curve is still not a certificate. The application must prove that its family dominates the required hypersurface, that the induced function-field extension is finite, and that a polynomial normal and compatible commuting derivatives give the integration identity. Affine cones illustrate the danger: every point lies on a radial line c(t)=tp, but the gradient of a sextic on that line is divisible by t^5. No polynomial vector \psi(t) can satisfy \nabla F(c(t))\cdot\psi(t)=1. Merely finding many such lines therefore supplies none of the required normal data.

An existence proof through finite field factors and trace can avoid expanding enormous coefficients. Extracting a practical denominator is a separate computational task. A curve degree or incidence dimension is not, by itself, the degree of the final cleared and homogenized denominator.

Nonvanishing at algebraic roots

A specialization can certify nonvanishing at every root of an auxiliary polynomial without selecting or specializing a particular root. Let R be a commutative ring, let K,L be fields, and let \phi,\psi be ring homomorphisms with

\phi:R\to K,\qquad \psi:R\to L, \qquad \ker\phi\subseteq\ker\psi.

For q,P\in R[t], suppose m>0, \deg q\le m, and

\psi(q)=u(t-r)^m,\qquad u\ne0,\qquad \psi(P)(r)\ne0.

Then \phi(P)(k)\ne0 for every k\in K satisfying \phi(q)(k)=0.

Proof. Set n=\deg P and form the resultant with fixed degree bounds m,n. Its Bézout identity shows that a common root of \phi(q),\phi(P) would force \phi(\operatorname{Res}_{m,n}(q,P))=0, and kernel inclusion would then force its specialization to vanish. But the coefficient-polynomial formula for this same resultant gives

\psi(\operatorname{Res}_{m,n}(q,P)) =u^n\psi(P)(r)^m\ne0.

Keeping the matrix sizes fixed makes this identity valid when P's degree drops at the specialization. ∎

All evaluations take place on the specified ring R, which can be a localization on which \psi is defined. The test may use a degenerate point where other coordinate formulas have poles: it evaluates polynomial covariants and their resultant, while those rational formulas are formed only after their own denominators have been proved nonzero. Neither irreducibility of q nor injectivity of the coefficient maps is required. The shared AlgebraicSpecialization module formalizes the criterion. Its quadratic application to the cubic shears of §2.9 follows fqlx's construction; its hypotheses preserve the distinction between specializing polynomial identities and evaluating rational maps.

5.5 Exact verification and Lean acceptance

For any proposed field v=N/D, normalized or otherwise, form the polynomial

E=A\cdot D^2-q\cdot D\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{5}N_i\cdot\partial_iF+F\cdot\left(D\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{5}\partial_iN_i-\sum_{i=0}^{5}N_i\cdot\partial_iD\right).

The required divisibility is equivalent to an exact polynomial identity

E=F^{q+1}\cdot Q

for some polynomial Q, together with the denominator and degree conditions. Indeed, E=D^2\cdot K_A(N/D), and D is invertible in the localization at (F). When the normalized equations hold with B_D(N)=F^q\cdot C, this gives Q=C.

A complete explicit check establishes:

  1. D\ne0, homogeneity of D, and \gcd(D,F)=1.
  2. Homogeneity of every N_i in degree \deg D+6\cdot q-5; zero components are allowed.
  3. The identity E=F^{q+1}\cdot Q with exact coefficients.

The identities may be checked in expanded, sparse or factored form. The representation changes the cost of the calculation, not the statement being proved. A strict certificate has Q=0. A nonzero Q is equally valid: the problem asks for divisibility, not a zero residual. If an even denominator and the standard component parities have been imposed during discovery, check those too; averaging can enforce them, so they must not narrow the underlying existence problem.

For an atlas submission, F,q,A are fixed by the protected instance record. Its standing smoothness and parity hypotheses are part of that target's preparation; a contributor does not replace the target while supplying a witness. A new instance, in contrast, needs those hypotheses checked separately.

Exact symbolic calculations are useful evidence and can guide the proof, but the live challenge requires a Lean theorem of certificate existence for that protected target. For an explicit construction, this means proving the displayed identity and its side conditions through the common certificate interface. A nonconstructive proof is also admissible if it establishes the same existential statement. Merely changing a status field or reporting a successful numerical or symbolic run does not establish formal acceptance.

An existence proof obtained through finite algebraic extensions need not present an expanded D. For example, the cover construction of §2.7 proves the existence of a finite curve-coefficient field, computes a residue primitive there, and descends it by trace before legal homogeneous clearing. Recovering a drawable or directly evaluable divisor is a separate extraction task. One can retain multiplication matrices and determinant or norm expressions rather than expand every coefficient, but must still identify the actual finite algebra and prove that its denominator remains nonzero on the physical hypersurface. An auxiliary cover equation or discriminant by itself is not automatically a certificate divisor.

A solve PR supplies the proof and a mathematical explanation of its construction. The protected checker verifies the theorem and its allowed axioms; the owner gives the final sign-off before merge.
