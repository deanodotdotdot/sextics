The geometric objective is to support the involution-invariant part of H^{4} of a smooth sextic fourfold on a divisor. The certificate equation translates this objective into an exact identity involving rational functions. Its advantage is that a proposed solution can be verified independently of the argument or numerical experiment that produced it. Its difficulty is concentrated in constructing the denominator.

The hypersurface and its targets

Write Z=(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},Y_{0},Y_{1},Y_{2}), let \tau (X,Y)=(X,-Y), and fix a homogeneous sextic F over a characteristic-zero field k\subset \mathbb C such that F(X,-Y)=F(X,Y) and X=V(F)\subset \mathbb P^{5} is smooth. A polynomial is Y-even or Y-odd according to the parity of its total degree in the three Y variables. Explicit records usually use k=\mathbb Q or a stated number field. Globally Y-even is the standing hypothesis; invariance under each individual coordinate sign change is a stronger condition used by particular constructions. The central target list uses integers q\in \{1,2\}; several constructions extend to other positive q with the same conventions.

We use Griffiths's residue theorem as a foundational input: residues of forms with pole order at most q+1 span F^{4-q}H^{4}(X,\mathbb C)_{\mathrm{prim}}, and their leading Hodge-graded quotient is the degree-6\cdot q piece of S/J(F), where S=\mathbb C[Z] and J(F) is the Jacobian ideal. This holds for 0\le q\le 4. Lower-pole numerators can be multiplied by F to put all forms over F^{q+1}. The residue and filtration statements, including their projective normalization, are presented in Diamond, Theorem 2.3.

Let \Omega =\iota _\epsilon (dZ_{0}\wedge \cdots \wedge dZ_{5}), where \epsilon =\sum _{i} Z_{i}\partial /\partial Z_{i} is the Euler field. Since \tau^{*}\Omega =-\Omega, the Griffiths form

\begin{gathered} \omega _A = A\cdot \Omega /F^{q+1}, \operatorname{deg} A = 6\cdot q, \end{gathered}

has invariant residue when A is Y-odd. Its residue lies in F^{4-q}H^{4}(X,\mathbb C)_{\mathrm{prim}}. In particular, q=1 presents the invariant (3,1) classes; q=2 presents the invariant Hodge-filtration level F^{2} and can also contain a contribution of type (3,1). The ambient class is the square of the hyperplane class and is already algebraic. There is no invariant (4,0) part, because \Omega changes sign. The desired generalized Hodge statement is

\begin{gathered} H^{4}(X,\mathbb Q)^\tau \subset N^{1}H^{4}(X,\mathbb Q). \end{gathered}

Here N^{1} denotes the rational subspace generated by classes vanishing off divisors. It is a Hodge substructure, by the Gysin description proved in Chapter 3. Solving all Y-odd targets for q=1,2 puts the invariant F^{2} part in N^{1}\otimes \mathbb C. Complex conjugation then puts its conjugate there too, and F^{2} plus its conjugate exhausts the invariant H^{4}. Taking supports for a finite spanning set gives one finite union of divisors supporting everything. Monomials form a convenient input basis, but targets may be arbitrary Y-odd homogeneous polynomials.

There is no loss in seeking an ambient equation D for such a support. The Lefschetz theorem on Picard groups gives \operatorname{Pic}(X)=\mathbb Z[\mathcal O_X(1)], so an effective divisor has class \mathcal O_X(e) with e>0. Its section lifts to a homogeneous polynomial D because H^{1}(\mathbb P^{5},\mathcal O(e-6))=0 in the restriction sequence. The lift is not a multiple of F. A \tau-stable support can be represented by the even polynomial D\cdot \tau (D), with repetitions allowed.

The key equation

For v=(v_{0},\ldots ,v_{5}), set v(F)=\sum _{i}v_{i}\partial _{i}F and \operatorname{div}(v)=\sum _{i}\partial _{i}v_{i}. A certificate for (F,q,A) is a rational field with homogeneous components of degree 6\cdot q-5, with every denominator coprime to F, satisfying

\begin{gathered} F^{q+1} \mid K_A(v), K_A(v) \coloneqq A - q\cdot v(F) + F\cdot \operatorname{div}(v). \end{gathered}

Divisibility is in the local ring k[Z]_{(F)}: clear the denominators, which are units there, and check polynomial divisibility. The required component parities are Y-odd for v_{0},v_{1},v_{2} and Y-even for v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}. Averaging with respect to \tau enforces these parities without changing the target.

The bridge to geometry is the rational four-form \beta =-\iota _v\Omega /F^q. Homogeneity makes it a projective form: its weight is zero and contraction with \epsilon is zero. Its differential satisfies

\begin{gathered} \omega _A - d\beta = K_A(v)\cdot\Omega /F^{q+1}. \end{gathered}

To prove the identity, put \mathrm{vol}=dZ_{0}\wedge \cdots \wedge dZ_{5}. Cartan's formula and \operatorname{deg} v_{i}=6\cdot q-5 give

\begin{gathered} d(\iota _v\Omega )=\operatorname{div}(v)\cdot\Omega -6\cdot q\iota _{v}\mathrm{vol},\\ dF\wedge \iota _v\Omega =v(F)\cdot\Omega -6\cdot F\iota _{v}\mathrm{vol}. \end{gathered}

Applying the quotient rule to \beta cancels the two terms containing \iota _{v}\mathrm{vol} and yields the displayed formula. The same calculation in \mathbb P^{n} for \operatorname{deg} F=\ell uses \operatorname{deg} v_{i}=q\ell -n and replaces 6\cdot q by q\ell.

The key equation therefore says that \omega _A-d\beta has no pole along X. On the complement of the denominator locus, the residue of an exact form and the residue of a form regular across X both vanish in cohomology, proving sufficiency. The prescribed-support theorem below proves the converse, together with an auxiliary pole bound, without leaving a separate algebraization step implicit. Diamond's Section 2 gives another necessity argument with a bound along X; that bound alone does not impose simple poles along the auxiliary divisor.

One denominator and the normalized equations

Every finite rational tuple has a common denominator. We may choose it Y-even, multiplying by its involution conjugate when necessary, and write

\begin{gathered} v=N/D, \operatorname{deg} D=d, \operatorname{deg} N_{i}=d+6\cdot q-5, \operatorname{gcd}(D,F)=1. \end{gathered}

A common denominator introduces no restriction on certificate existence. The quotient rule gives

K_A(N/D) = [D\cdot(A\cdot D-q\cdot N(F)) + F\cdot(D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N)-N(D))]/D^{2}.

For this fixed D, existence is equivalent to the following polynomial system:

\begin{gathered} E_{1}: q\cdot N(F)=A\cdot D,\\ E_{2}: D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N)-N(D) \in (F^q). \end{gathered}

The forward implication is an Euler normalization, not an extra ansatz. Indeed, the key equation first forces A\cdot D-q\cdot N(F)=F\cdot H for a polynomial H. Replacing N by N'=N+H\epsilon /(6\cdot q) makes E_{1} exact. Since \operatorname{deg} H=d+6\cdot q-6, Euler's identity also gives

\begin{gathered} D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N')-N'(D) = D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N)-N(D)+D\cdot H, \end{gathered}

which is precisely the expression already required to lie in (F^q). Degrees and parities are preserved.

For fixed D, both equations are linear in the coefficients of N. One can compare coefficients after reducing E_{2} modulo F^q, or introduce a quotient polynomial and impose an exact identity. Thus the unrestricted fixed-denominator question is finite linear algebra. Restricting N to a convenient smaller space changes that question and requires a separate completeness argument.

A certificate is strict when K_A(v)=0 as a rational function. In normalized form this means E_{1} holds and E_{2} vanishes identically, rather than merely modulo F^q. Strictness is a property of the chosen field; ordinary solvability does not require it.

Euler freedom and denominator gauges

For every homogeneous rational \phi of degree 6\cdot q-6,

\begin{gathered} q(\phi\cdot\epsilon )(F)=6\cdot q\phi F=F\cdot \operatorname{div}(\phi\cdot\epsilon ). \end{gathered}

Consequently v and v+\phi\cdot\epsilon have the same K_A. An Euler shift preserves strictness as well as ordinary solvability. With \phi Y-odd it also preserves parity. The preceding normalization uses \phi =H/(6\cdot q\cdot D), whose poles remain legal.

There are also formally invisible Euler fields with poles on X, such as (P/F)\epsilon for \operatorname{deg} P=6\cdot q. These are not legal certificate fields: the coprimality requirement forbids their denominator. A coefficient search which admits D proportional to F can therefore detect spurious solutions. Homogeneity and small residual alone do not certify legality.

One further gauge is useful for sextic denominators at q=1:

\begin{gathered} (D,N) \mapsto (D+t\cdot F, N+t\cdot A\cdot \epsilon /6). \end{gathered}

It preserves E_{1} and E_{2} modulo F, and V(F,D+t\cdot F)=V(F,D). It need not preserve strictness, since it changes the rational field itself. Coprimality must still be checked. This gauge is distinct from adding an Euler field to a fixed v.

Indeed, put B_D(N)=D\cdot \operatorname{div}(N)-N(D). For q=1 and \operatorname{deg} D=6, the shifted numerator has N'(F)=A(D+t\cdot F). Euler's identity gives \operatorname{div}(A\cdot \epsilon /6)=2\cdot A and (A\cdot \epsilon /6)(D)=A\cdot D. Expanding then gives

B_{D+t\cdot F}(N+t\cdot A\cdot \epsilon /6) = B_D(N)+t\cdot F(\operatorname{div} N+t\cdot A),

using E_{1} to cancel the remaining terms. This proves precisely the stated modulo-F invariance.

Prescribed support and pole multiplicity

The support of a divisor ignores multiplicities; a rational primitive does not. These facts must be kept separate when fixing a denominator.

Prescribed-support theorem. Let D be nonconstant, homogeneous, Y-even and coprime to F, and let D_{\mathrm{red}} be a squarefree equation of V(D). For 1\le q\le 4, the following statements are equivalent:

  1. \operatorname{Res}_X(\omega _A) vanishes on X\setminus V(D).
  2. A certificate exists with poles confined to V(D).
  3. A certificate exists with denominator D_{\mathrm{red}}^q.

In particular, D^q also suffices. Over the field of definition of F,A,D, the numerator can be chosen over that same field. The field can be chosen with the prescribed component parities. With the even presentation D^q, its numerator has those same parities; a squarefree equation D_{\mathrm{red}} can itself be odd, in which case its numerator transforms accordingly. No smoothness or normal-crossing hypothesis is imposed on V(D) or its intersection with X. Without a field-parity requirement, the same equivalence applies to arbitrary homogeneous D.

We prove the slightly more general statement for a smooth degree-\ell hypersurface X\subset P=\mathbb P^{n}, with n\ge 2, 1\le q\le n-1 and \operatorname{deg} A=\ell (q+1)-n-1. Put R=V(D_{\mathrm{red}}), e=\operatorname{deg} D_{\mathrm{red}}, and work over \mathbb C until the final linear descent. We use unshifted de Rham complexes, with differential forms in degrees 0,\ldots ,n.

The external inputs are the following standard facts about mixed Hodge modules and projective space. They are stated here to specify exactly what the proof uses.

  • Localization along a reduced divisor T carries a Hodge filtration \mathcal F with \mathcal F_{-1}\mathcal O_P(*T)=0 and \mathcal F_{k}\mathcal O_P(*T)\subset \mathcal O_P((k+1)T) for k\ge 0; equality holds when T is smooth. Inclusions and quotients of these filtered modules are strict. See Mustaţă–Popa, Hodge ideals, equation (0.1) and §§4–7.
  • Projective direct image is strict: for a filtered mixed Hodge module M on projective P, the map from the hypercohomology of \mathcal F^p\operatorname{DR}(M) to that of \operatorname{DR}(M) is injective. Here its j-th term is \Omega ^{j}_P\otimes \mathcal F_{j-p}M. See Mustaţă–Popa, Hodge ideals for \mathbb Q-divisors, §1 and equation (1.1).
  • Algebraic de Rham comparison identifies localization with the derived direct image from the divisor complement; this is the comparison input used in Diamond, §2.3. Bott vanishing gives H^{i}(P,\Omega ^{j}_P(b))=0 for b>0 and i>0, as recalled in Hodge ideals, §25; line bundles on \mathbb P^{n} also have zero H^{1} for n\ge 2.

Proof. Set

\begin{gathered} M=\mathcal O_P(*(X+R))/\mathcal O_P(*R). \end{gathered}

The residue comparison is \mathbb H^{n}(P,\operatorname{DR}(M))\cong H^{n-1}(X\setminus R,\mathbb C), with the usual residue Tate shift. To see the shift, work first on P\setminus R with a local normal coordinate t for the smooth X. Modulo regular forms, all higher normal poles are exact and the only normal de Rham class is \mathrm dt/t, one degree higher than the corresponding class on X. Localization along R extends this residue identification to P. Since X is smooth, \omega _A belongs to the top term of \mathcal F^{n-q}\operatorname{DR}(M): it comes from \mathcal F_q\mathcal O_P(*X)=\mathcal O_P((q+1)X). If its residue vanishes, strictness of projective direct image says that its class already vanishes in \mathbb H^{n}(P,\mathcal F^{n-q}\operatorname{DR}(M)). This is the only step that requires more than unfiltered de Rham exactness.

For n-q\le j\le n put a_j=j-n+q+1 and introduce the finite complex

\begin{gathered} K^{j}=\Omega ^{j}_P\otimes [\mathcal O_P(a_j(X+R))/\mathcal O_P(a_jR)],\\ K^{j}=0 \;\text{for}\; j<n-q. \end{gathered}

Differentiation increases each pole order by at most one, so it gives the differential of K^{\bullet}. The Hodge pole bound provides a map of complexes \mathcal F^{n-q}\operatorname{DR}(M)\to K^{\bullet}. To check this on quotients, a filtered representative has poles at most a_j along both X and R. A representative lying in \mathcal O_P(*R) therefore lies in \mathcal O_P(a_jR). Strictness identifies precisely those representatives with \mathcal F_{a_j-1}\mathcal O_P(*R), so the quotient map is well defined. Consequently the class of \omega _A is zero in \mathbb H^{n}(P,K^{\bullet} ).

Each K^{j} has zero higher cohomology. Indeed, in

\begin{gathered} 0 \to \Omega ^{j}_P(a_jR) \to \Omega ^{j}_P(a_j(X+R)) \to K^{j} \to 0 \end{gathered}

both twists are positive because a_j\ge 1 and e>0. Bott vanishing and the long exact sequence give the claim. Hence \mathbb H^{n}(P,K^{\bullet} ) is computed by global sections. The zero class of the top section \omega _A supplies a global section \overline{\beta } of K^{n-1} with d\overline{\beta }=\omega _A. The same sequence in degree n-1, and H^{1}(P,\Omega ^{n-1}_P(q\cdot R))=0, lift it to

\begin{gathered} \beta \in H^{0}(P,\Omega ^{n-1}_P(q\cdot X+q\cdot R)). \end{gathered}

Thus \omega _A-d\beta has poles only on R. In particular, the primitive has at most q-fold poles along every component of R and along X, with no added support.

It remains to obtain the vector field, rather than merely assert that a rational form can be represented. The identity \Omega ^{n-1}_P\cong T_P(-n-1) and the twisted Euler sequence give

0 \to \mathcal O_P(q(\ell +e)-n-1) \to \mathcal O_P(q(\ell +e)-n)^{n+1} \to \Omega ^{n-1}_P(q\cdot X+q\cdot R) \to 0.

The left term has zero H^{1}. Therefore \beta is represented by a homogeneous polynomial tuple N of degree q(\ell +e)-n:

\begin{gathered} \beta =-\iota _N\Omega /(F^q\cdot D_{\mathrm{red}}^q). \end{gathered}

With v=N/D_{\mathrm{red}}^q, the differential identity proved above converts the absence of an X-pole into the key equation. This proves 1\Longrightarrow 3. The implication 3\Longrightarrow 2 is immediate, and 2\Longrightarrow 1 is the residue argument already given. Multiplying N by (D/D_{\mathrm{red}})^q gives the prescribed denominator D^q. Averaging the field under \tau preserves this even denominator and the target, and enforces the component parities. It can also retain D_{\mathrm{red}}^q, since stability of its reduced support implies D_{\mathrm{red}}\circ \tau =\pm D_{\mathrm{red}}. Finally, the fixed-denominator normalized equations form a finite linear system over the original coefficient field. Consistency over \mathbb C implies consistency over that field by Gaussian elimination. ∎

This also proves the claimed necessity of certificates for an arbitrary divisor support: choose an ambient D cutting that divisor, apply the theorem, and use the resulting polynomial tuple. If the original coefficients lie in a smaller field, the descent argument below gives a certificate over that field, possibly with a different support. The proof bounds poles on a chosen support; it supplies no bound on the degree e of the support itself.

At q=1, therefore, existence over D is equivalent to existence over any D^{m}. An exact failure of the full fixed-D linear system excludes every higher power of that D. At q=2, the definitive test on its support uses D^{2}. Failure over D alone need not exclude support.

The distinction is sharp. Set

\begin{gathered} D=X_{0}^{6}+X_{1}^{6}+X_{2}^{6}, E=Y_{0}^{6}+Y_{1}^{6}+Y_{2}^{6}, F=D+E,\\ A=X_{0}^{3}\cdot X_{1}^{3}\cdot X_{2}^{3}\cdot Y_{0}\cdot Y_{1}\cdot Y_{2}, q=2. \end{gathered}

Writing \epsilon _X and \epsilon _Y for the two block Euler fields, the strict certificate is

\begin{gathered} v=A(F\epsilon _X-D\cdot \epsilon _Y)/(12\cdot D^{2}). \end{gathered}

For the numerator N_{2}=A(F\epsilon _X-D\cdot \epsilon _Y)/12, direct blockwise differentiation gives

\begin{gathered} 2\cdot N_{2}(F)=A\cdot D^{2}, \operatorname{div} N_{2}=A\cdot F, N_{2}(D^{2})=A\cdot F\cdot D^{2}. \end{gathered}

These identities prove both normalized equations, with E_{2} identically zero. The field has degree seven and the required parities. Fermat smoothness is immediate from its partial derivatives, and D is coprime to F because D and E involve disjoint variables and have no common factor.

There is no numerator over D. To prove this without an ansatz, normalize E_{1} and average a hypothetical numerator over the six independent sixth-root coordinate scalings, projecting onto the character of A. Both F and D are invariant, so this operation preserves the fixed denominator and the two equations. The retained monomials in N_{i} have exponent vector congruent modulo six to (3,3,3,1,1,1)+e_{i}. These six residues are all less than six and already sum to the required numerator degree thirteen. Thus N_{i}=c_{i}AZ_{i} is the entire surviving space, not a selected family. E_{1} forces c_{0}=c_{1}=c_{2}=1/12 and c_{3}=c_{4}=c_{5}=0. For this numerator, \operatorname{div} N=A and N(D)=A\cdot D/2, so E_{2} is A\cdot D/2. It is not divisible even by F. This excludes every original numerator because normalization and averaging preserve existence.

These bounds leave the central search problem untouched: they do not bound the degree of the reduced support that must be found.

Combining certificates and descending coefficients

For fixed F and q, the equation is linear in the pair (A,v). If v_j serves A_j, then \sum _{j}c_j\cdot v_j serves \sum _{j}c_jA_j. A product of the denominators supplies a common denominator, so supported target spaces add when supports are united. A decomposition into symmetry characters can therefore organize a search without requiring one denominator to serve every component separately.

In cleared form, certificates (D,N,Q) for A and (E,M,R) for B combine as

D_{A+B}=DE,\qquad N_{A+B}=EN+DM,\qquad Q_{A+B}=E^2Q+D^2R.

The resulting defect is E^2\mathcal E_A+D^2\mathcal E_B=F^{q+1}(E^2Q+D^2R). Nonzero quotient terms therefore cause no obstruction to addition. Denominator legality is preserved by products, and for positive q each numerator has degree \deg D+\deg E+6q-5. The shared Lean module HodgeComputer.CertificateOperations proves this operation for every positive pole order, including existential certificates.

If F and A are rational and v is defined over a number field K, normalized trace gives a rational certificate:

\begin{gathered} v_\mathbb Q = \operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(v)/[K:\mathbb Q]. \end{gathered}

To prove descent, sum the conjugate fields over all embeddings of K into a normal closure. Differentiation commutes with each embedding, so each conjugate serves the same A. Their average is Galois-invariant and hence rational. The product of the conjugate denominators is the norm, clears that average, and has degree [K:\mathbb Q]\operatorname{deg} D before cancellation. Each factor is coprime to F, so the norm remains legal. Homogeneity and parity are unchanged.

The same argument works for every finite extension K/k in characteristic zero. It also suffices to descend an initially complex certificate for data over k. Fix its denominator degree and write the normalized equations in the finitely many coefficients of D,N and the E_{2} quotient. These are polynomial equations over k. Legality is an open condition: at least one coefficient of the remainder of D modulo F is nonzero. Choose such a coefficient and invert it using one additional variable. A complex point of this finite polynomial system implies a point over an algebraic closure of k, by the Nullstellensatz; its finitely many coordinates generate a finite extension. Trace then gives a certificate over k. This proves descent of existence, without claiming to preserve an initially transcendental supporting equation.

For a bundled target A+cB, with 1,c linearly independent over \mathbb Q, the nondegenerate trace pairing supplies p\in K satisfying \operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(p)=1 and \operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(p\cdot c)=0. Indeed, evaluation of that pairing on the independent vectors 1,c defines a surjective linear map K\to \mathbb Q^{2}. Applying trace to the certificate identity for p\cdot v shows that \operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(p\cdot v) serves A. More generally a trace-dual basis extracts every rational component of an extension-field target. Trace over passive function fields appears in Chapter 2, where commutation with the relevant derivatives is proved for that setting.

For completeness, character averaging also has an explicit formula. Suppose a finite diagonal group acts by \gamma :Z_{i}\mapsto \gamma _{i}Z_{i}, with F\circ \gamma =\mu _\gamma F and A\circ \gamma =\nu _\gamma A. Define

\begin{gathered} (T_\gamma v)_{i}=\mu _\gamma (v_{i}\circ \gamma )/(\nu _\gamma \gamma _{i}). \end{gathered}

The chain rule gives K_A(T_\gamma v)=K_A(v)\circ \gamma /\nu _\gamma. Thus averaging T_\gamma v preserves the certificate and projects onto the required character. A common denominator is the product of the conjugates, so its degree may grow by the group order. This does not justify imposing a single character on D at a previously chosen degree.

There is a useful exponent description when F contains all six pure sixth powers. Let L(F) be the lattice generated by differences of exponents in the support of F. Diagonal substitutions preserving F up to scalar are exactly the characters of \mathbb Z^{6}/L(F). Modulo global scalar substitutions this is a finite group: the six pure powers force all \gamma _{i}^{6} to agree, and a common scalar makes them all one. Characters separate its exponent cosets at fixed degree. For a character field component, reduced numerator and denominator are both eigenvectors: the identity obtained by transforming their coprime quotient forces each factor to be carried to a scalar multiple of itself. Their characters differ by that of A times the i-th coordinate divided by the character of F. This proves the support-coset rule without discarding rational fields, though averaging may increase degree.

For a full-support Y-even sextic, L(F) is exactly the lattice of vectors of total sum zero and even Y-sum. One inclusion follows from parity. Conversely, exponent differences realize all within-X and within-Y coordinate transfers, and also 2(e_{X_{i}}-e_{Y_{j}}); these generate that lattice. Hence all Y-odd targets of one degree belong to a single coset. Fine target-character decompositions are a property of structured F, not an available simplification for general F.

Jacobian reduction and exact verification

Let J(F)=(\partial _{0}F,\ldots ,\partial _{5}F). If A=\sum _{i}G_{i}\partial _{i}F, taking v=G/q gives

\begin{gathered} K_A(G/q)=F\cdot \operatorname{div}(G)/q. \end{gathered}

This lowers the pole order by one: modulo an exact form, \omega _A becomes (\operatorname{div} G/q)\Omega /F^q. Membership in J(F) therefore kills the leading Hodge-graded piece; it need not kill the whole residue class. For invariant q=1 targets it does kill the class: the lower contribution would lie in the anti-invariant (4,0) space. Equivalently, after parity averaging, \operatorname{div}(G) is a Y-odd constant and hence zero.

For a concrete q=2 example in our diagonal involution coordinates, take Fermat F and

\begin{gathered} A=6\cdot X_{0}^{6}\cdot X_{1}^{3}\cdot X_{2}^{2}\cdot Y_{0}, G_{0}=X_{0}\cdot X_{1}^{3}\cdot X_{2}^{2}\cdot Y_{0}, G_{i}=0 \;\text{for}\; i>0. \end{gathered}

Then A=\sum G_{i}\partial _{i}F is Y-odd and belongs to J(F), but one reduction gives the q=1 numerator X_{1}^{3}\cdot X_{2}^{2}\cdot Y_{0}/2. All of its exponents are at most four, so it is nonzero in the Fermat Jacobian ring \mathbb C[Z]/(Z_{0}^{5},\ldots ,Z_{5}^{5}). Griffiths's graded isomorphism makes its (3,1) class nonzero. Thus the original q=2 residue is nonzero, despite Jacobian membership. A complete reduction algorithm must retain this lower-pole target.

For an explicit field, clearing denominators reduces verification to polynomial identities and the homogeneity and coprimality conditions. A nonzero remainder is allowed when it is divisible by F^{q+1}; strictness is not an acceptance requirement. Chapter 5 gives the complete identity and explains how these algebraic checks enter a Lean proof of the protected instance statement.
